## Independent, and clear about where that ends

I don't sell software, I don't resell services, and I take no commission from any supplier or
vendor. When I recommend something, the only reason is that I think it's right for you.

That sounds like a small thing until you've sat on the other side of a process where it wasn't
true. Recommendations quietly bend toward whoever has a relationship with the advisor. Evaluation
criteria get written to favour a known answer. It's rarely dishonest — it's gravity, and the only
reliable defence is an advisor with nothing to gain either way.

I'm also clear about the one place I'm not neutral. Implementation delivery needs a team, and where
that's what a client needs I work in partnership with EAMS Global, a preferred global implementation
partner for Fracttal. There's no revenue share in that relationship. And I don't act as client
representative on a programme delivered by a partner I take resource work from — those two roles
stay separate.

An independence claim is only worth something if it's specific enough to be checked.

## Engineering rigour, applied to commercial problems

I trained as an industrial and mechanical engineer, and I've never stopped working that way.

Engineering assumes the answer is discoverable if you measure the right things. Applied to sourcing
and to technology programmes, that means starting from what's actually happening rather than what
people believe is happening — real spend, real service levels, real system usage, real data
quality. Most of the disagreement in these projects dissolves once everyone is looking at the same
evidence.

It also means the process is designed before it runs. Evaluation criteria are agreed before
responses arrive. Acceptance criteria are agreed before deliverables are reviewed. That discipline
is what makes a position defensible when someone senior challenges it six weeks later — and someone
always does.

## The person you meet is the person who does the work

There's no team behind me to hand this to. The person who scopes your engagement is the person
running the evaluation, sitting in the supplier negotiation, and writing the report.

That's a genuine constraint as well as a benefit: I take on fewer engagements than a firm would,
and I'll tell you if I'm not the right fit or can't start when you need me. What you get in
exchange is 25 years of judgement applied directly to your problem, rather than a methodology
applied by whoever was available.

## Long relationships, by preference

Several client relationships have run six years or more, through multiple renewals. I mention it
not as a credential but because it says something about how I work.

Advisory that ends at the recommendation is easy to deliver and often worthless. The value shows up
during mobilisation, when the contract meets reality — or two years in, when the assumptions behind
a decision need revisiting. I'd rather do fewer engagements properly and stay useful afterwards
than optimise for the next proposal.

## How a sourcing engagement runs

**Assess** — Establish what's actually happening. Current spend, service levels, contract
structure, and the constraints nobody wrote down.

**Strategize** — Agree what good looks like and how to get there, before going to market. Sourcing
strategy, commercial model, and the evaluation framework, settled up front.

**Source / Facilitate** — Run the process. RFI, RFP, evaluation, and negotiation, conducted
transparently enough that you can see how each conclusion was reached.

**Support mobilization** — Stay through transition. This is where a good decision either survives
contact with reality or doesn't, and it's the phase most often under-resourced.

**Assure performance** — Make sure the contract delivers what it promised, and stay available when
the assumptions behind it need revisiting.

Not every engagement needs all five. Some clients bring me in with strategy already settled; some
want only the assessment. But the sequence is the same, and skipping the first two is the most
common reason these projects disappoint.

## How technology work runs

Technology engagements don't follow that shape, and I won't pretend they do.

They start wherever you are. A programme already underway needs an independent technical voice in
governance from the next steering meeting onwards. A deployment that isn't delivering needs
assessment before anything else. A partner team needs functional capability from the point they're
short of it.

What stays constant is the standard. Design decisions get challenged before they're locked in.
Deliverables get measured against acceptance criteria agreed in advance, not judged on
presentation. Data readiness is demonstrated rather than asserted — the single most common cause of
a stalled go-live is data that everyone assumed was fine.

The one rule I hold to is separation of roles. I'm either on the client's side of a programme or on
the delivery team. Never both on the same programme.

## What I don't do

Being clear about this saves us both time.

- I don't implement systems as SynPro. Implementation needs a team; I join one
- I don't provide managed services or ongoing system administration
- I don't resell software or take vendor commissions
- I don't represent a client on a programme delivered by a partner I work with
- I don't take engagements where the answer is already decided and the advisor is there for cover
